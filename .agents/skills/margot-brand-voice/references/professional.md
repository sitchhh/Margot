# Professional / investor

Sound like a prepared founder speaking directly to another person: warm, clear,
and confident about the purpose of Pink Fitness Club. Let specific evidence do
the persuading. These are initial writing guidelines for Magnolia to refine.

## Writing choices

- Lead with why this message matters to this recipient. Use a researched or
  supplied connection; leave out flattering guesses about their interests.
- Explain the customer need and business concept plainly. Keep women's comfort,
  belonging, and practical needs visible in the story.
- Use short paragraphs, natural contractions, and one clear next step. Avoid
  pitch-deck jargon, inflated superlatives, performative enthusiasm, and emojis
  by default. A familiar correspondent can still receive a warmer note.
- For prospective investors, make the relevance and invitation clear. For current
  investors, focus on confirmed progress, what remains unresolved, and any useful
  request. Do not reintroduce the whole business in every update.
- Be candid about setbacks and uncertainty. Don't turn a plan into a completed
  milestone or imply that historical community support proves profitability.
- Keep investment terms, returns, deadlines, and amounts out unless confirmed
  for this message. A polished sentence must not make an estimate sound settled.

## Same message, professional version

Illustrative copy using the historical Kickstarter result in the project brief;
not a complete email or Magnolia-approved wording:

> Pink Fitness Club's Kickstarter campaign closed with $30,488 pledged by 320
> backers, exceeding its $30,000 goal. Thank you for supporting the vision of a
> full-service gym designed around women's needs. That support is meaningful
> as we work toward opening.

Use this acknowledgment only for an actual supporter. For a new prospect, the
same result can supply context without implying an existing relationship. The
casual profile expresses the same facts with a different rhythm.

For a hypothetical delay, once that delay is confirmed, prefer a direct statement
such as “We don't have a confirmed opening date yet. I'll share it once it is set.”
over reassuring language that implies certainty or progress without evidence.
